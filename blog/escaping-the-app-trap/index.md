  <h1 style="font-size:3em;">Escaping the App Trap</h1>
  <h2 style="font-weight:normal; color:grey;">Redefining success in an attention economy.</h2>
  <p style="color:gray;">{{ page.date | date: "%B %-d, %Y" }}</p>
  <div style="max-width:800px; margin:0 auto;">
    <img src="/blog/escaping-the-app-trap/header.png" alt="Illustration of a person coding under a spotlight while a sea of notifications and likes swirl around them." style="max-width:80%; height:auto; padding:16px; border-radius:24px;">
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:24px;">
      For years I’ve been building iOS apps — some in Flutter, others in SwiftUI — and I still get a rush every time I see an idea come to life on screen. There’s nothing like the thrill of hitting <em>Run</em> and watching the simulator bloom to life with something you created from thin air.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      But somewhere along the way, a quiet voice started whispering: <em>“Wait a minute — am I part of the problem?”</em>
    </p>
    <h3 style="margin-top:32px; font-weight:700; font-size:2em;">🧩 When Building Apps Starts to Feel Like Feeding the Machine</h3>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:12px;">
      The modern app economy doesn’t really reward usefulness anymore. It rewards <strong>attention</strong> — the currency of the distracted age. The more eyeballs you hold, the more “successful” you are. Every notification, every “one more thing before you go,” is designed to stretch attention just a little further.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      I’m not even on TikTok or Instagram because I find the whole attention dynamic kind of bleak. Yet there I was, making apps that used the same principles to survive. It felt like standing outside the casino criticizing the gamblers while selling them energy drinks through the window.
    </p>
    <h3 style="margin-top:32px; font-weight:700; font-size:2em;">🤔 The Ethics of Enjoying the Craft</h3>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:12px;">
      I love building apps. The problem is, the ecosystem sometimes feels designed to turn every developer into a <em>mini attention merchant</em>. Marketing, that necessary evil, revolves around capturing attention and refusing to let go.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      At some point I had to ask: <strong>is there a way to keep making apps without feeling like I’m manipulating people?</strong>
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      Turns out — yes, but you have to be deliberate. You start by <strong>redefining success</strong>. Instead of chasing engagement graphs that look like EKGs, you focus on how well your app <em>serves</em> someone. You make tools that help people <em>finish</em> and <em>log off</em>. You charge money honestly instead of selling eyeballs. You join humane tech communities like IndieWeb, Center for Humane Technology, or Small Tech Foundation — the rebels of the attention wars.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      In short, you build things that make people’s lives a bit <em>quieter</em>.
    </p>
    <h3 style="margin-top:32px; font-weight:700; font-size:2em;">🎯 Marketing Without the Manipulation Hangover</h3>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:12px;">
      Once I made peace with ethical app design, a new villain appeared: marketing. Because if you want your creation to survive, you have to get people to notice it.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      So I started reimagining what “marketing” could mean. Maybe it’s not about <strong>grabbing</strong> attention but about <strong>earning</strong> it. Maybe it’s less about virality and more about <em>trust</em>.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      Here’s what I found works:
    </p>

        <tr>
          <th style="text-align:left; padding:8px 12px; border-bottom:1px solid #ddd;">Approach</th>

      <tbody>
        <tr>
          <td style="padding:8px 12px; vertical-align:top;">Transparency</td>
          <td style="padding:8px 12px;">Share how and why you built the app. People love seeing the craft.</td>

        <tr>

        </tr>
        <tr>
          <td style="padding:8px 12px; vertical-align:top;">Community</td>
          <td style="padding:8px 12px;">Connect with like-minded builders rather than chasing influencers.</td>
        </tr>
        <tr>

    </table>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:12px;">
      Sure, it’s slower. But it builds credibility the algorithm can’t fake. And the people who come in through that door? They tend to stay.
    </p>
    <h3 style="margin-top:32px; font-weight:700; font-size:2em;">📈 The MRR Mirage</h3>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:12px;">
      Of course, the attention economy has a cousin: the <strong>metrics economy</strong>. I’ve watched my RevenueCat dashboard like a nervous stock trader, measuring “success” by how much I made in the last 30 days.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      Spoiler: <em>no number is ever high enough.</em> That’s when I realized I was chasing the same validation loop my apps were trying to free users from — different screen, same dopamine.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      So I built a new kind of dashboard — one that measures <strong>meaning</strong> as well as <strong>money</strong>.
    </p>

    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:12px;">
      It’s amazing how liberating it feels when <em>“enough” becomes a metric too.</em>
    </p>
    <h3 style="margin-top:32px; font-weight:700; font-size:2em;">🌱 Building in a World That Wants Your Attention</h3>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7; margin-top:12px;">
      So here’s where I’ve landed: I’m still making apps, but I’m building <strong>against the gravity</strong> of the attention economy. My apps might live in the same ecosystem as the addictive ones, but they orbit differently.
    </p>
    <p style="font-size:1.35em; line-height:1.7;">
      Because being a developer isn’t just about clean code. It’s about deciding <em>what kind of world your code belongs to.</em> And if I can make things that give people back even a sliver of calm, agency, or self-respect — then maybe that’s the best kind of “growth” there is.
    </p>
    <h3 style="margin-top:32px; font-weight:700; font-size:2em;">⚙️ TL;DR</h3>
    <ul style="text-align:left; margin:12px auto 24px; max-width:680px; font-size:1.15em; line-height:1.6;">
      <li>Don’t build for <strong>attention</strong> — build for <strong>intention</strong>.</li>
      <li>Don’t market for <strong>clicks</strong> — market for <strong>trust</strong>.</li>
      <li>Don’t measure <strong>revenue alone</strong> — measure <strong>meaning</strong>.</li>
    </ul>
